    Ipse dixit

    Ipse Dixit or why "because the tool/vendor/expert/artificial intelligence said so" is not evidence.

    A digital forensics expert witness holds immense power, within a legal setting. They translate complex digital structures, volatile memory captures, and encrypted logs into narratives that judges and/or juries can understand. However, as tools become more automated and proprietary, the digital forensics and incident response (DFIR) community faces a growing vulnerability: the logical fallacy of ipse dixit.

    Ipse dixit, is Latin for "he himself said it", is a legal and philosophical term for an assertion that is accepted as true blindly, resting entirely on the authority of the speaker rather than on objective logic or independent verification. In digital forensics, ipse dixit manifests when an analyst (or examiner) states that a technical artifact exists or means a specific thing solely because a tool, or its documentation, or training says so.

    The Trap of Tool-Centric Reliance

    Modern forensic tools parse complex file systems and other data structures, such as the Windows Registry, at the click of a button. But they are not infallible. Bugs, parsing errors, and outdated signatures can lead to incorrect findings.

    When an analyst copies a report directly from a commercial tool into their forensic affidavit without verifying the underlying data, they are relying on an ipse dixit. If pressed on the witness stand during cross-examination about how the tool reached that conclusion, an answer of "because the tool said so" will cause the evidence-and the expert's credibility-to crumble. The judge is not interested in the vendor's unverified authority; the court requires scientific validity.

    The Daubert Standard vs. The Closed Source

    In jurisdictions governed by the Daubert Standard, expert testimony must be the product of reliable principles and methods. A key pillar of this standard is peer review and testability.

    Commercial, closed-source tools present a unique ipse dixit challenge. Because their proprietary source code is a closely guarded trade secret, the defense cannot directly audit how a specific artifact was parsed. Therefore, the forensic analyst must bridge this gap. They must avoid treating the vendor's documentation (including training) as an infallible text. Instead, they must validate the findings using open-source utilities, manual analysis, or controlled replication of the user activity.

    Best Practices for Defeating the Fallacy

    To ensure your digital forensic findings withstand scrutiny and remain entirely free of ipse dixit bias, integrate the following practices into your standard operating procedures:

    • Cross-Tool Verification: Cross-validate critical artifacts using at least two independent tools, ideally combining a commercial suite with a trusted open-source tool. Make sure the tooling is independent; there are known examples of tool authors adopting erroneous practices from other tools.
    • Manual Verification: If necessary go down to the byte level. If a tool claims a timestamp indicates a specific file creation time, manually parse the Master File Table (MFT) or Windows Registry key to confirm the logic holds true. This is especially important for conclusions about implied user behavior.
    • Maintain a Testing Environment: If a vendor claims their software can accurately trace a specific application's telemetry, recreate the scenario on a clean virtual machine. Document the results to provide empirical proof, substituting an unverified claim with actual data.

Thus, his deposition testimony is simply too little too late.

    Conclusion

    Output of tooling is a starting point for an investigation, and should never be the final destination. By actively resisting the comfort of ipse dixit, digital forensic analysts protect the integrity of their science, fulfill their duty to the court, and ensure that justice relies on verifiable facts rather than assumptions.
